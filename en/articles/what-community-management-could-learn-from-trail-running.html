What community management could learn from trail running

August 3, 2026 · 2 min read

A community manager spends their career trying to create live emotion, spontaneous conversation, and a sense of belonging. On a trail race finish line, all of that happens naturally, with no content strategy and no editorial calendar.

A finish line at five in the morning

It's 5am at a finish line. The winner crossed long ago, filmed, interviewed, celebrated. But the crowd is still there, for the very last finisher, the one closing out the race after twenty-eight hours of effort. They get applauded louder than the winner did. People sometimes cry. Nobody scheduled this moment. It simply happened, because the community present understood what it meant.

Why this scene is worth more than a campaign

This moment holds, condensed into a few seconds, the three ingredients every community manager tries to reproduce online:

  • The live moment. Nothing replaces the unscripted present. GPS tracking and live commentary on a race produce the same adrenaline rush as a well-run social media livestream, except here the stakes are real.
  • Raw emotion. The best content is never the kind that tries to provoke emotion. It's the kind that captures it at the moment it already exists. A finish line is full of it, with no special effects needed.
  • A community that tells its own story. Runners, volunteers and spectators become, without ever being asked, the event's best ambassadors. They post, comment, and share, not because a brand told them to, but because they lived through something worth telling.
You don't build a community by publishing content. You create content because a community already exists.

Responsiveness also matters more than people think: 73% of consumers say they'll buy from a competitor instead if a brand fails to respond to them on social media (Sprout Social, Social Media Statistics 2026). This isn't just a hunch from the field. Studies on customer loyalty tell the same story: an already-loyal customer is roughly five times more likely to buy again than a new prospect, and loyalty can boost a brand's profitability by up to 95% (Antavo, Customer Loyalty Statistics 2026). Community management knows this, even if it doesn't always act on it: brands invest heavily to attract people, and far less to make them want to stay. Trail running does the opposite, without even realizing it.

Personal takeaway

Many brands try to "build community" the way they launch a campaign: with a budget, a calendar, reach targets. Trail running reminds me, race after race, that community can't be decreed. It's cultivated, over time, by genuinely showing up for the moments that matter to the people who make it up. It's a lesson in humility for anyone who works in communications.

What's a community moment you witnessed that no brand could ever have scripted?
