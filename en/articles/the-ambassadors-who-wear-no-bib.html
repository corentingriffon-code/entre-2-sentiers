Why a race's best ambassadors sometimes wear no bib at all

August 3, 2026 · 2 min read

On a trail race, there are the people who run, and there's everyone else. The volunteers at the aid stations, the locals who set up a table and cups outside their homes, the local associations that mark the course for weeks beforehand. No bib, no finish time, no finish line to cross. And yet, they're often the event's best ambassadors.

Three in the morning, a headlamp

On an isolated stretch of trail, at three in the morning, a volunteer has been holding a headlamp for six hours to light up a technical section. He doesn't know the runners passing in front of him. No official camera will ever film him. But it's often him, and thousands like him, that runners remember longest — far longer than the logo on their finisher t-shirt.

What an army of volunteers really represents

This is community marketing in its purest form, and it deserves to be called that. To give a sense of scale: a major urban marathon commonly mobilizes between 1,500 and more than 3,000 volunteers to staff the course, the aid stations and the route markings (Detroit Free Press Marathon, Marine Corps Marathon). These aren't extras. They're a volunteer communications force, larger than the marketing team of most brands that sponsor these races.

  • Volunteers embody the brand better than any campaign, because their commitment is selfless and therefore credible.
  • Locals turn a place the race merely passes through into a place that welcomes it — a shift in status that completely changes how the event is perceived, both for runners and for residents.
  • Local associations root the race in a real ecosystem, beyond sport, and create communication channels that no media budget could buy.

The organizations that succeed best don't just "recruit volunteers." They value them, thank them, and put them forward as their brand's first ambassadors — because that's simply what they are. It's the same logic as the user journey designed for every runner: a coherent organization takes care of everyone's experience, not just the end customer's.

A brand can buy visibility. It cannot buy the trust inspired by a selfless volunteer.

Personal takeaway

This subject strikes a particular chord with me because it's a reminder of something we too often forget in communications: the best social proof is never the kind you stage — it's the kind that already exists, sincerely, all around us. The communicator's role isn't to manufacture it. It's to know how to see it, and to give it room to tell its own story.

Do you have a memory of a volunteer, at a race or an event, that stuck with you more than the race itself?
