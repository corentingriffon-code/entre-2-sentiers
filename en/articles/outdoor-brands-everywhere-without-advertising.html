Why are some outdoor brands everywhere without ever looking like advertising?

September 28, 2026 · 4 min read · Photo: Rémi Blomme

There are outdoor brands we talk about constantly, that we run into at every race start line, in every conversation between trail runners, without ever feeling like they're selling us something. And there are others that pile on campaigns, partnerships and posts, without ever really managing to enter the conversation.

The difference isn't about budget. It's about precision.

It's not a question of volume, but of precision

We tend to think visibility can be bought: the more you communicate, the more you're seen. But it isn't the quantity of messages sent that builds a brand, it's their ability to precisely reach the audience you're trying to reach. A brand that says one single thing, but says it to the right person, at the right time, with sincerity, can have more impact than a brand that communicates nonstop without ever really leaving a mark.

This is especially true in the outdoor world, where the audience is small, passionate, and spots anything that rings false immediately.

Patagonia: one message, never diluted

Patagonia is probably the most obvious example. The brand almost never communicates head-on about its products. It tells, over and over, the same story: repair rather than repurchase, the environment rather than growth at any cost. The message doesn't vary, it doesn't get diluted in generic product content. And it's precisely that consistency that makes it credible.

COROS and Salomon: talking to runners, not the general public

COROS and Salomon follow a different but complementary logic. These brands don't try to speak to everyone. They speak to runners, through the athletes they sponsor, present on the ground rather than on a broad media plan. The message is narrow, almost confidential to anyone outside trail running, but it reaches exactly the intended audience, at the moment they're paying attention.

Nnormal: the brand that announced it wouldn't do marketing

The most radical example is probably Nnormal, the brand launched by Kilian Jornet. At launch, Kilian Jornet explicitly announced that the brand wouldn't do any particular marketing, relying solely on a handful of international trail runners as guarantors of its image. No media plan, no classic launch campaign. Just a handful of voices the target audience deemed legitimate.

It's not the loudest voice that gets heard. It's the one that says the right thing, to the right person.

What research says about advertising effectiveness

This isn't just an impression. The work of Les Binet and Peter Field for the IPA, the UK's reference body on advertising effectiveness, draws on hundreds of campaigns analyzed over several decades. Their conclusions point the same way: emotionally strong, memorable campaigns generate nearly twice as much profit growth over the long term as purely informational campaigns.

Even more striking: according to their work, campaigns awarded for their creativity, judged particularly on-point and memorable, are about eleven times more effective at growing market share than non-awarded campaigns. In other words: the precision of a message multiplies its effect, while a forgettable message wastes most of the investment that paid to spread it, even with a sizeable budget behind it.

HOKA, the exception that proves the rule?

There is, however, an interesting counter-example within the outdoor world itself: HOKA. In its early days, the brand grew almost entirely through word of mouth among ultra-trail runners, who recommended these then-oversized-looking shoes to each other. Little communication, but total precision with a convinced niche.

Today, HOKA has become one of the most visible brands on the circuit, at the heart of the marketing battle playing out around UTMB Mont-Blanc. Does this "less but right" model only work for an emerging brand, before it has the means to communicate at volume? Or is it a choice even a large, established brand could keep making? The question is worth asking.

Personal takeaway

What I take from these examples is that a brand's presence isn't measured by how often it speaks up, but by how precisely it chooses what to say, to whom, and why. Patagonia, COROS, Salomon and Nnormal don't need to do a lot to be seen, because they know exactly who they're talking to and what they have to say.

And that might be the real question to ask before launching a campaign: not "how do I get seen more", but "what actually deserves to be said, and to whom".

And you, which outdoor brand has stuck with you by saying very little, but exactly the right thing?
