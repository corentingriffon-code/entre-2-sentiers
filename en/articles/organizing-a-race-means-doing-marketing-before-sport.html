Organizing a race means doing marketing before doing sport

August 3, 2026 · 3 min read

The first meeting to organize a trail race almost never talks about sport. It talks about positioning.

The meeting that isn't about sport

Before a single course marker is placed on a trail, an organization has to answer questions that have nothing to do with sport: who is this race for? What image do we want to project — demanding, family-friendly, community-driven, prestigious? Which partners do we want to associate with that identity, and which ones do we turn down despite their check? What story do we want runners to tell once they're back home?

The course route, the rules, the race categories — all of that comes after. The race exists first as a brand, before it exists as an event. And the financial stakes are far from trivial: the global sports sponsorship market topped 70 billion dollars in 2025, driven precisely by events capable of selling far more than a finish line (AMW, Sports Marketing Statistics 2026).

Three decisions that shape everything else

Three decisions, made months before the starting line, determine everything else:

  • Positioning. A race that's "popular and accessible" and a race that's "demanding and prestigious" don't recruit the same runners, don't speak to the same partners, and don't communicate the same way. Positioning means choosing who you exclude as much as who you attract.
  • The audience. An event designed for elite athletes and one designed for beginners don't share the same tone, the same visuals, or the same channels. Many races fail by trying to speak to everyone at once.
  • The story. A race that sells only distance and elevation gain misses the point. The organizations that last are the ones that sell a story: the story of a place, a community, of collective achievement.

This logic extends well beyond sport: 66% of consumers say they're more likely to buy a product after interacting with a brand at an in-person event (Freeman, Commerce Trends Report 2025). A trail race, without knowing it, ticks that exact box: it's a full-scale, real-world brand activation.

A race doesn't sell 80 kilometers. It sells a story.

Why we really sign up for a race

The route? Sure. The distance? Obviously. But not only that. We also sign up for the story we'll get to tell afterward: running in a mythical place, crossing a finish line surrounded by thousands of people, wearing a bib we've waited months for, sharing a weekend with the people we love, being able to say one day "I was there."

A race doesn't just sell 30, 50, or 100 kilometers, then. It sells an experience, a world, emotions, an identity. The biggest outdoor events understood this a long time ago: their communication almost never talks only about distance or elevation gain. It tells stories of landscapes, encounters, personal breakthroughs, community.

This is probably one of the great strengths of sports marketing: not just making people want to take part in an event, but making them want to belong to it. That's also why a race bib can become far more than a piece of paper.

Personal takeaway

This is probably the angle that fascinates me most about trail running: seeing how much the best organizations think like brands long before they think like event organizers. The stopwatch comes at the end. The strategy starts months earlier, in a meeting room, over a map, with one simple question: what story are we going to tell?

Which race made you want to belong to its story, rather than just take part in it?
